DESIGNING FOR FEELING

Actions, pace, and comfort.

My interface and design reflection · updated November 16, 2026

01 / My intention and current prototype

I am developing Desktop Beomgyu, an unofficial photo-based companion that I want to feel playful and comforting. I want visitors to choose an action, adjust his pace, and experiment with Quiet comfort to find the kind of company they want.

I created this website as an interactive preview alongside my downloadable Mac prototype. I am currently refining the website; I have not yet added its newest controls and animation changes to the Mac build.

02 / How I combine inputs

I use different input types for different decisions. I chose radio buttons for one action at a time, a slider for continuous speed adjustment, and a checkbox for a comfort setting that can combine with any action.

Control I choseAvailable stateWhat I designed it to do
Action radio buttonsStay, Walk, Run, Celebrate, HugI let visitors choose one action at a time.
Movement speed slider50% to 180%; default 100%I let visitors adjust walking and running pace continuously, with a percentage that updates immediately.
Quiet comfort checkboxOn or offI make walking and running slower, Hug gentler in timing, and the celebration bounce smaller.
Size radio buttonsTiny, Small, LargeI let visitors choose how large the character appears.
Reset to Stay buttonInitial controlsI provide a clear way back to Stay, Small, Quiet comfort off, and 100% pace.

I combine the action, slider, and checkbox rather than treating them as unrelated effects. For example, I can compare Run at 150% with Quiet comfort off and on to explore how the same action can feel energetic or calmer. I limit the speed slider to Walk and Run so its purpose stays clear.

I use this simplified logic to describe how my inputs affect the response:

action change → select behavior
slider input → update pace and percentage
checkbox change → apply comfort response

if action is Walk or Run:
  speed = baseSpeed × (slider / 100) × (quiet ? 0.55 : 1)
if reduced motion is preferred:
  stop wandering

Reset → Stay + Small + 100% + comfort off

03 / My structure and layout choices

I organize the preview, controls, Trash-helper explanation, and download into semantic HTML sections. I use fieldsets, legends, and labels to make the input groups understandable. With AI assistance, I use CSS Grid for a two-column desktop layout and stack the content at tablet and mobile widths.

I enlarged the labels and numbered the controls after people found the interface difficult to read or use. I added a response card to describe the selected action and comfort setting, while the slider shows its own percentage. I also kept keyboard focus outlines so visitors can see which control they are using.

Today, I noticed a large blank area beneath the preview because the controls column had become taller. I asked AI to tighten the spacing and keep the preview visible beside the controls while scrolling. I wanted visitors to see Beomgyu respond while adjusting the lower settings.

04 / How I collaborated with AI and revised the project

I supplied the concept, reference photos, original hug GIF, and design feedback. I used Codex as a technical and creative partner to help implement HTML, CSS, JavaScript, photo preparation, and documentation. I made decisions by comparing its output with the movement and feeling I wanted, and I did not accept every result.

How I protected the original appearance

Earlier in the project, I rejected a generated hug because the face no longer looked like Beomgyu. I asked AI to keep his face unchanged, remove the background, and preserve the motion. I kept the approach that uses 20 original GIF frames with background masks instead of redrawing his face. I continued to require the original face during today’s animation revisions.

What I corrected in the animation today

I noticed that walking and running looked like sliding because the arms and legs did not alternate convincingly. I asked for opposite arm and leg movement. I also pointed out that he lingered in the same area, so I requested continuous wandering with changing directions rather than repeated pauses or a fixed route.

When the animation changed, I saw the walking shoulder and running elbow separate from the body. I supplied screenshots and challenged the attempted fixes because the gaps were still visible. I asked AI to look at the actual problem. AI then rebuilt the arm connections using photo-textured meshes with shared joint edges and separate upper-arm and forearm movement. I treated these changes as an experiment that still needed visual review.

I also clarified that arm layering and arm swinging were different issues. I did not want an arm to jump from behind the torso to in front of it during a stride. For running, I specifically asked AI to reverse the existing arm layers, then keep that order fixed. After that looked closer to my intention, I separately requested opposite arm-leg timing for walking and then running. These exchanges helped me describe a visual problem more precisely instead of only saying that the motion looked awkward.

What I added to the interaction today

For the module’s focus on combined inputs, I asked to add another UI element from the assignment’s list. AI implemented a Movement speed slider from 50% to 180%. I kept it because it connects directly to the character’s behavior and combines with the action radio buttons and Quiet comfort checkbox. I can change the pace gradually, then turn on Quiet comfort to slow that chosen pace further.

I also asked for the large blank area in the layout to be adjusted. AI tightened the desktop spacing and made the preview stay beside the controls while scrolling. Finally, I asked for the project information and reflection to include the new slider and recent revisions, and for this process page to use my first-person perspective.

I learned that AI can help implement a revision quickly, but it can also misunderstand a visual request or claim a fix before the result matches my intention. My role was to identify the mismatch, give concrete feedback, distinguish motion from layering, and keep refining the interaction.

05 / What I checked and what remains limited

I asked AI to check the slider at 50% and 180%, combine it with Quiet comfort, and confirm that Reset returns it to 100%. I also asked it to inspect several points in the stride and check the desktop layout while scrolling. I continued to review the visible results and point out problems; I do not consider these checks a guarantee of flawless motion on every device.

I use the website to preview the interaction, so it does not access, move, or delete visitors’ files. I explain the Trash-helper sequence as a native app feature. I provide the Mac prototype and character assets in one ZIP; that build requires Apple silicon and macOS 26 or later and is not notarized. I have not yet brought today’s website changes into that build. I am also interested in ChatGPT integration, but I have not implemented it.

06 / What I am learning and sharing

I learned that HTML structures my content, CSS controls its layout and style, and JavaScript functions and event listeners let my interface respond to combined states. I am exploring how movement, timing, and feedback can communicate feeling. I am still considering how strong the difference between energetic and quiet responses should be before it becomes distracting.
